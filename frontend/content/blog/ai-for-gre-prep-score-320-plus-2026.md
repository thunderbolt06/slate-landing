The GRE has not changed much in the last decade. The way smart people prepare for it has.

A serious 320+ scorer in 2026 does not sit in a four-month classroom course. They do not grind through a 600-page book front to back. They run very tight feedback loops with an AI tutor that knows their exact weaknesses, drills them on the patterns they get wrong, and adapts the next set of questions to push them just past their current ability. This guide is about how to do that without falling into the traps most people fall into.

## Why the textbook approach is slow

A standard prep book is the same book for every student. If you are already at a 165 on Quant, a chapter on "how to multiply fractions" is wasted time. If you keep missing parallelism questions, a generic Verbal chapter will not give you 30 reps on parallelism specifically. The book treats every reader as average. Almost nobody is.

The GRE rewards specificity. Specificity in what you study, specificity in what you drill, specificity in how you review wrong answers. The faster you make every hour of prep about exactly the question types you are weakest on, the faster your score moves. That is the whole game.

## Step 1: Take a real diagnostic before you study a single thing

The single biggest mistake people make is starting to "study" before they know what their actual weaknesses are. Spend a Saturday morning on the official PowerPrep diagnostic. Take it under real conditions, untimed sections off, full length, no breaks longer than the real ones.

When you finish, do not just look at your score. Look at the question-by-question breakdown. Group your misses into categories. For Quant: arithmetic, algebra, geometry, data interpretation, quantitative comparison. For Verbal: text completion, sentence equivalence, reading comprehension, critical reasoning. Count your misses in each bucket. Two or three buckets will dominate. Those are your study plan for the next two weeks. Nothing else.

This is the part where AI starts to be useful. [Try Slate at slateup.ai](https://slateup.ai) and feed it your score breakdown. Ask it to build you a two-week plan targeted at your bottom three buckets. The plan will be better than anything in a prep book because it is for one person, you.

## Step 2: Drill in tight loops

Once you know your weak buckets, the next mistake is doing a 50-question practice set and reviewing it at the end. Too long a loop. By question 50 you have forgotten what you were thinking on question 12.

Run shorter loops. Five questions, then review. Five more, then review. The AI tutor's job during review is to ask you what you were thinking, not just tell you the right answer. The right answer alone is not useful. Knowing why you picked the wrong one is.

A good AI tutor will ask things like: when you read the question, did you try to predict the answer first or did you go straight to the choices? Did you eliminate any options? What made you commit to your final pick? These are the questions a strong human tutor would ask. Most students never get them because most students cannot afford a strong human tutor.

## Step 3: Build a wrong-answer log

Every wrong answer goes in a log. Question type, why you missed it in one sentence, and what the right approach would have been. Keep this in a single document.

Re-read it every morning before you study. Most people skip this. Their score moves slowly because the same mistake keeps showing up. The students who jump 8 to 12 points all do something like this.

Slate's AI classmates can help here too. After a drill session, describe the questions you missed out loud and have the AI summarize the pattern in your errors. Often the pattern is not what you think. You might believe you are bad at geometry. The AI might point out that you actually have geometry down but you keep misreading the question stem. That is a completely different fix.

## Step 4: Vocab is overrated. Mostly.

There is a cult around GRE vocab. Magoosh's deck, Manhattan's lists, Anki for 1500 words. Most of this is a waste of time.

The Verbal section tests reading more than it tests vocabulary. If you read three or four hard nonfiction articles a week, your vocabulary will pull itself up. A short list of 200 high-frequency words is worth memorizing. The other 1300 are diminishing returns. Skip them.

If you must memorize, use spaced repetition and only review the words you have actually seen on a practice question and missed. Words you have never seen in a real GRE-style passage will not show up either.

## Step 5: Quant is about pattern recognition, not math

The GRE Quant section does not test math past tenth grade. It tests whether you can recognize that a problem disguised as a word problem is actually a ratio problem you have seen 30 times before. Or a backsolve problem. Or a "plug in a number" problem.

The fastest way to build that pattern recognition is to do 20 problems of the same type back to back. Then take a day off. Then do 20 more. Spaced repetition for problem types works better than spaced repetition for vocabulary.

This is exactly the kind of drill an AI tutor can run that a textbook cannot. Ask Slate to generate 20 GRE-style ratio problems at increasing difficulty. Solve them. Review them with your AI classmates. Identify the patterns. Move on.

## Step 6: Reading comprehension is a separate sport

Most GRE prep treats reading comprehension as just another question type. It is not. It is a distinct skill that takes weeks to build and almost no one practices correctly.

The correct practice is not doing more GRE passages. It is reading 20-minute articles from The Economist, The Atlantic, or peer-reviewed humanities papers, and forcing yourself to summarize each one in three sentences before moving on. Do this for a month. Your RC score will move more than from any number of practice passages.

The reason GRE RC feels hard is that the passages are deliberately dense. If your normal reading life consists of social posts and short Substack notes, your brain is not trained for that density. The fix is to put dense material back into your daily reading.

## Step 7: Mocks, two weeks out

In the last two weeks before the test, run a full-length mock every three to four days. Untimed off, real conditions. Most students stack mocks too close together at the end and burn out. Two days of recovery between mocks is non-negotiable.

After each mock, do a long review. Take an entire day if you have to. The review is the test prep, not the mock itself. The mock just generates the wrong answers that fuel the review.

## Common mistakes that cap your score

Starting too early without a diagnostic. Three months of prep with no plan beats four months with no plan, but a six-week sprint with a sharp plan beats both.

Doing too many sources. Manhattan, Magoosh, Princeton Review, Kaplan, all at once. Pick one set of materials plus the official ETS material. Stop.

Skipping the wrong-answer log. The single highest leverage habit in test prep.

Treating Verbal as a vocab problem. It is a reading problem with a vocab tail.

Cramming the week before. Your score is locked in by ten days out. The last week is sleep, light review of the wrong-answer log, and arriving fresh on test day.

## The score is a side effect

The students who jump from 308 to 322 are not the ones who study the most hours. They are the ones who study the right hours with sharp feedback loops. AI makes it cheaper and faster to run those loops, but the loop has to exist in the first place.

The actual goal is not the score. The actual goal is to genuinely understand the patterns this test rewards, in a way that would let you tutor someone else through it. If you can do that, the score takes care of itself.
