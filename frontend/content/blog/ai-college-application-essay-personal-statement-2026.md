The college essay is the strangest part of the application. Your GPA is a number. Your test scores are numbers. Your activities are a list. The essay is a 650-word window into who you actually are, and somehow it carries more weight than any single grade.

In 2026, AI has changed how students approach this. Most use it badly. A few use it well. This guide is about how to use it well.

## What admissions officers can actually tell

Let us start with the elephant in the room. Yes, admissions officers can usually tell when an essay was written by AI. They read thousands of essays each year. AI-written essays have a specific texture. They tend to be smooth, well-organized, polished, and completely forgettable. They sound like a TED talk written by a corporate communications team.

What admissions officers want is the opposite. They want specific. Strange. Honest. A real moment, not a general theme. A real voice, not a generic one. Your goal with AI is not to outsource the writing. It is to outsource the parts of the process that slow you down so you can spend more time on the parts that matter.

## The right way to use AI: as a thinking partner

The best application of AI to your college essay is not at the writing stage. It is at the thinking stage.

Most students sit down to write an essay before they have figured out what the essay is actually about. They have a vague topic, maybe a moment they want to describe, but they have not yet found the deeper thing the essay needs to say. That is where AI is genuinely useful.

[Try Slate at slateup.ai](https://slateup.ai) — when you describe a story you are thinking about writing, your AI classmates can ask the kinds of questions a good college counselor would ask. What were you actually feeling? What did you learn? Why does this moment matter to you? What would your life look like if this had never happened? These questions force you to find the real essay underneath the surface story.

A practical workflow: spend 20 minutes describing your top three essay ideas out loud, with AI asking follow-up questions. By the end, you usually know which one has the most depth. That is the one to write.

## How to brainstorm topics without writing 50 first drafts

A common mistake is trying to brainstorm by listing topics in a notebook. The list always sounds the same. Family. A hard class. A summer job. A grandparent.

A better approach is to brainstorm by memory. Open a conversation and just describe specific moments that stuck with you, with no goal except to remember them. The time you missed the bus and ended up walking home with someone you barely knew. The first time you got something wrong in front of your whole class. The conversation with your mom that you still think about. The hour you spent rebuilding something that broke.

AI can help you go deeper on each one. What do you remember most clearly? What was the temperature of the room? What was your physical body doing? What were you thinking but not saying? The essays that work are full of these small details. Most students skip them and go straight to the takeaway. The takeaway is the least interesting part.

## Drafting: write it yourself first

This is the part people get wrong. Once you have a topic and the key moments, write the first draft yourself. By hand if you can. Without AI involved at all.

The first draft will be bad. That is the point. You are trying to find your voice, not produce a finished product. The first draft is where you discover what you actually think. If AI writes the first draft, you will never have that discovery, and the essay will feel borrowed for the rest of its life.

Set a timer for 25 minutes. Write the whole essay top to bottom in one sitting. Do not edit as you go. Do not check word count. Just write.

## Revision: where AI earns its keep

Now AI is useful again. Paste your draft into a conversation and ask three specific questions.

First: what is unclear? Not what is bad. Just what does not make sense. AI is good at finding sentences that almost mean something. These are the sentences you need to rewrite.

Second: what is missing? Are there moments that feel rushed? Are there places where you skip from one feeling to another without showing the transition? A good revision pass adds, it does not subtract.

Third: what does this essay actually say? Have AI summarize your essay in two sentences. If the summary is something like "the writer learned the importance of perseverance," your essay is too generic. Real essays summarize as specific paradoxes or contradictions. "The writer hated their grandmother's silence and now uses it every day" is a real essay summary.

## What to never do

Do not let AI write a sentence and keep it. The texture is different. Admissions officers can feel it.

Do not use AI to "make it sound more sophisticated." Sophistication is not what they are looking for. Honesty is.

Do not use AI to "make it stand out." Standing out comes from being specific, not from being clever. AI tends to push toward cleverness.

Do not ask AI to "write it in my voice." It cannot. It will produce a generic version of how a thoughtful student is supposed to sound. That is not your voice. That is everyone's voice.

## Subject-specific tips

**Common App essay**: this is the broad personal statement. The prompts are intentionally open. The best essays usually do not directly answer the prompt at all. They tell a story and let the meaning emerge. Use AI to help you find the story, not to answer the question.

**Why this school essays**: these are the easiest to do badly. Most students list classes, professors, and clubs. The good ones explain how you specifically would use the school, and why this school in particular would change what you do. AI can help you research specifics. Do not have it write the answer.

**Activity descriptions**: 150 characters per activity. The temptation is to pack in achievements. The better approach is to pack in verbs. What did you actually do? AI is useful here for tightening. Write the long version, then have AI help you cut without losing specificity.

**Supplemental essays**: shorter, weirder prompts. UChicago's prompts are the most extreme example. These essays are the place to take real risks. AI is not great for this. It pulls toward the safe answer. If a prompt is weird, lean into the weirdness yourself.

## A practical schedule

If you are starting from scratch in May for an October deadline, here is the timeline that works.

May to June: brainstorm. No drafts. Just thinking, writing fragments, and noticing what comes back.

July: first drafts of the main essay. Three or four versions of completely different topics, not three drafts of the same topic.

August: pick one essay. Revise it five to ten times. Use AI in the revision pass, not the drafting pass.

September: write supplemental essays. These usually take less time than the main essay if your main essay is strong.

October: have a teacher, a parent, and someone outside your family read everything. Make final edits. Submit.

## A real example of AI used well

Imagine a student writing about their grandfather, who died when they were ten. The temptation is to write a moving tribute. Most of these essays are bad because they are about the grandfather, not the writer.

The student uses AI to brainstorm. AI asks: what is one small thing you still do because of him? The student thinks for a while and answers: I still set out three chairs at dinner even though we are four people. AI asks: have you ever counted? How long has it been? The student does not know.

That becomes the essay. Not a tribute. A 650-word meditation on a habit the student did not know they had until they were asked. The essay does not even fully explain itself. It does not need to. The reader understands.

This is what AI is for. It asks the question that opens the door. The student walks through it themselves.
